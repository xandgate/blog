---
title: "I Wrote a Book Chapter About My Startup. Then It Failed."
subtitle: "Being right about the problem and wrong about the solution"
summary: "In 2021 I wrote a chapter for an academic book about a startup I was building. The startup didn't make it. Here's what that feels like five years later."
publishedAt: "2026-08-02"
---

In 2021, I wrote a chapter for an academic book called *Intelligent Economies: Developments in the Caribbean*. The chapter was about my startup, Farm Credibly — a platform using blockchain to help Jamaican farmers access financing.

Farm Credibly didn't work. Not in the way I described in the chapter.

The book is still out there. My name is on the chapter. And five years later, I'm still not sure how to feel about having written it.

---

The thesis was real. Jamaican farmers can't access loans — not because they're bad credit risks, but because there's no data to assess their creditworthiness. Banks can't afford to verify a subsistence farmer in St. Elizabeth. The farmer doesn't have records. The loan amount is too small to justify the due diligence. So agriculture contributes over 7% of Jamaica's GDP and receives 2% of its commercial loans. Eighty-nine percent of farmers can't borrow.

Blockchain, I argued, would fix this. Every transaction verified on a distributed ledger. No bank required as intermediary. Farmers could build a verifiable credit history through their existing trading relationships — the agro-processors they sold to, the input suppliers they bought from. Smart contracts would prequalify them for financing. Investors would fund vetted farms through a crowdfunding model.

It was a clean story. It had a historical parallel I was proud of — Luca Pacioli's double-entry bookkeeping in 1494, which enabled modern finance by building trustworthiness into record-keeping. I was saying blockchain was the next leap. The same structural insight, four hundred years later.

The problems I identified were real. Every single one. That part I'd stand behind today.

The solution was wrong.

---

A blockchain doesn't create records that don't exist.

Most of the transactions that would prove a farmer's creditworthiness happen in the informal economy. Cash sales at market. Trades with neighbours. Payments to day labourers. None of that has a digital footprint. A distributed ledger can verify what's already recorded. It cannot capture what was never written down.

The crowdfunding model ran into Jamaican securities law. You can't let investors fund farms and share profits without regulatory approval that takes years and money we didn't have. The technology was the easy part. The compliance was the wall.

And the adoption problem was the most humbling of all. Asking Jamaican subsistence farmers to manage digital wallets and interact with smart contracts — in 2021, in rural parishes with intermittent data connectivity — was asking people to solve a technology problem they didn't have in order to solve a financing problem they did. The barrier was backwards.

We pivoted. Explored an AI chatbot that could speak Jamaican Patois, answer questions about planting seasons and market prices, meet farmers where they actually were. That's still interesting to me. But it's a different thing entirely from what I wrote about in the chapter.

---

Here's what I've been sitting with: there's a difference between writing about what you've learned and writing about what you hope will be true.

I wrote the second one and called it the first.

The chapter was published in good faith. The research was real. The data was real. The historical parallel was genuinely interesting. But I was presenting Farm Credibly as evidence of transformational change while the transformation hadn't happened. I was the case study author and the subject at the same time, and I didn't have the distance to see that those were different jobs.

If the startup had succeeded, the chapter would be a marketing document. Because it failed, the chapter is a diagnostic record — a snapshot of what a thoughtful person believed about blockchain and agriculture before reality corrected them.

That's actually more valuable. I believe that. But it took five years to believe it without also feeling embarrassed.

---

The problems are still there.

Farmers in St. Elizabeth still can't get loans. Jamaica still imports onions while the soil grows them fine. The average farmer is 60 years old and there's no one coming behind them. The hot pepper export market grew 713% between 2013 and 2017 — real demand, real capacity — and most of the farmers who could have supplied it couldn't access the capital to scale up.

I didn't solve any of that. Farm Credibly didn't solve any of that. The chapter documented an attempt that didn't work.

What I'm more certain of now than I was in 2021: the problem was never the technology. It was always the information. Farmers and banks and buyers are all making rational decisions on incomplete data. The market doesn't self-correct because the participants can't see each other. You don't fix that with a ledger. You fix it by making information flow.

What that actually looks like — whether it's an AI that speaks Patois, or structured data from voice conversations, or something else entirely — I don't know yet. I know the blockchain wasn't it.

Maybe that's the honest version of what the chapter was trying to say.
